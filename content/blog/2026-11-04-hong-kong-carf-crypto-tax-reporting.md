Hong Kong's Legislative Council passed its crypto tax reporting bill on November 4, 2026. The [government announcement](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202611/04/P2026110400310.htm) says covered service providers must begin collecting information next year, ahead of exchanges with partner tax authorities starting in 2028.

The legislation implements the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework, or CARF, alongside amendments to the Common Reporting Standard. It covers more than buying and selling coins for cash. The legislative text includes transfers of relevant crypto-assets and tokens representing property or other rights, bringing the reporting rules into the services through which users move assets into and out of on-chain finance.

## Collection starts before international exchange

The [bill published by the Inland Revenue Department](https://www.ird.gov.hk/eng/pdf/aeoi/Inland%20Revenue%20(Amendment)%20(Crypto-Asset%20Reporting%20Framework%20and%20Amended%20Common%20Reporting%20Standard)%20Bill%202026_Eng.pdf) puts the CARF provisions into operation on January 1, 2027. The government confirmed that providers will collect the information specified by that framework during 2027. The department will begin exchanging it with partner jurisdictions from 2028. Providers must assemble their records from the collection date; the later international exchange date is the deadline for a different part of the process.

The amended Common Reporting Standard has a separate timetable. It takes effect in 2028, followed by automatic exchanges of financial account information under the updated standard from 2029. The government says those amendments cover digital financial products and enhance reporting and due-diligence requirements. Crypto-asset transaction reporting and financial-account reporting will follow these different implementation schedules under the legislation.

Hong Kong says the exchanges will be reciprocal and subject to data confidentiality and security. The statutory definitions distinguish partner jurisdictions from reportable jurisdictions, with the Commissioner able to specify each through published lists. The framework uses the jurisdiction where a customer is resident for tax purposes. A wallet's location alone does not establish that residence.

## Who falls within the provider definition

The legislative text defines a reporting crypto-asset service provider as an individual or entity that carries on a business executing exchange transactions for or on behalf of customers. It expressly includes acting as the counterparty, acting as an intermediary and making a trading platform available. A platform assessing whether it falls within that definition must look at the service it provides, regardless of its branding.

A separate set of nexus rules connects the provider to Hong Kong. The listed connections include Hong Kong tax residence, an entity managed from Hong Kong and a regular place of business there. Incorporation or organisation under Hong Kong law is another connection when the entity has local legal personality or an obligation to file tax returns or tax information returns relating to its income.

A Hong Kong branch can also bring a provider within the framework when relevant transactions are executed through that branch. Under this rule, the obligations apply to the transactions executed through the Hong Kong branch. The text sets out this branch connection separately from the residence, incorporation, management and place-of-business rules that apply to the provider itself.

The bill also provides conditional relief from local due diligence for some entities with overlapping jurisdictional connections. An entity connected through incorporation, management or a regular place of business can qualify when it meets the specified requirements in a partner jurisdiction because it is tax-resident there. The provision also requires it to furnish a return under the Hong Kong framework. An entity must consider that return requirement separately from its eligibility for due-diligence relief.

The customer definition has its own qualification. When an agent, nominee, signatory, investment adviser or other listed intermediary acts for another person, the text generally treats that underlying person as the crypto-asset user. The provision excludes financial institutions and reporting crypto-asset service providers from this particular look-through rule. For a business using an intermediary, both the underlying person's identity and that qualification determine whether the named account operator is the relevant user.

## Transfers and tokenized claims are in scope

The asset definition includes cryptographic tokens representing membership claims, property rights and other rights that can be digitally exchanged for fiat currency or other crypto-assets. It covers categories used in tokenized finance without limiting the framework to a list of named cryptocurrencies. The narrower definition of a relevant crypto-asset then applies exclusions to determine which of those assets fall under CARF.

Those exclusions cover central bank digital currencies, specified electronic money products and assets that a reporting provider adequately determines cannot be used for payment or investment. A token's payment function is part of the scope test, as are its legal rights and redemption terms. To qualify as electronic money under the definition, an asset must meet conditions beyond being a digital representation of a currency.

The listed electronic-money conditions include issuance on receipt of funds for payment transactions, a claim against the issuer denominated in the same fiat currency and acceptance by someone other than the issuer. Regulatory requirements must also make the asset redeemable on request, at any time and at par, for that currency. Providers must check these conditions when classifying a product. The text does not name individual stablecoins as qualifying products.

A relevant transaction can be an exchange or a transfer. The transfer definition covers movements to or from a user's crypto-asset address or account where the provider cannot determine, from the information available at the time, that the transaction is an exchange. It excludes an account maintained by that provider for the same user. Moving assets to an external wallet can fall within the transaction categories even when the user is not selling them.

The government says the Inland Revenue Department has established a dedicated team to answer industry questions and provide technical support. The department will issue implementation guidance, but the passage announcement neither supplies that guidance nor resolves the classification of individual tokens and platforms.
